About

Background & research question

The question I’m chasing: how can digital environments be designed to optimize the real, physical spaces we live in and the lives we live inside them?

Researcher building digital 3D environments and generative, AI-assisted design systems, with a background in architecture, landscape design, and VR research into how space is experienced. That background, built during a Master’s at Istanbul Technical University, first raised the question above.

Architectural project work spans generative, algorithm-driven form and performance-integrated design, including passive-cooling facades and stormwater and carbon-management strategies, alongside VR research on depth perception and environmental legibility in head-mounted displays, published at eCAADe and submitted to CAADRIA.

That question now drives research at Simon Fraser University’s iViz Lab, combining generative AI-assisted design with physiologically adaptive, biofeedback-driven interaction — methodology now extending into a paper in preparation on AI in architectural design. Also teaches graphic design and 3D animation at SFU.

Submitted, ACM SIGGRAPH 2026 · not accepted

Embedding Physiological Responsiveness into Game World Dynamics

BioTerrarium · Unity, Apple Watch heart rate & HRV · within-subjects study, n = 24

A closed-ecosystem game environment whose fog, vegetation, and light respond in real time to a player’s heart rate and heart rate variability, read from an Apple Watch over Bluetooth/HealthKit and streamed into Unity at under 100 ms latency. A resting baseline normalizes each player into one of four physiological zones, from Coherent Calm to Dysregulated Overdrive, each mapped to a distinct environmental state.

In a within-subjects study against a fixed-schedule control, the bioadaptive condition produced lower heart rate, higher HRV, close to 80 percent of playtime in the two calmest zones, and higher self-reported mindful attention and flow.

Submitted to ACM SIGGRAPH 2026; went through a full review cycle and was not accepted. Still evaluating where this line of research fits next.

In revision, ACM · resubmitting 2027

Reading a Remote Teammate’s Body

Remote collaboration · ambient physiological awareness · ACM

Extends the ambient-reciprocity principle behind BioTerrarium into synchronous remote collaboration — the screen-sharing, co-design, or joint problem solving that increasingly happens with a teammate present only through a call — asking whether that teammate’s focus, strain, or calm can be conveyed ambiently instead of displayed as a chart.

A companion browser study validated how well people can interpret ambient heart-rate and HRV icon cues without numerical values, across a randomized within-subjects recognition task.

Submitted to an ACM venue; reviewers asked for a revise-and-resubmit, meaning the core idea held up but the paper needs another pass. Resubmission planned for 2027.

Published · eCAADe 2023

Creating Immersive Virtual Landscapes

Virtual Landscape Interface X Lab · depth perception & environmental legibility

Master’s research studying how people actually perceive depth and distance inside a head-mounted display — a virtual landscape can look right and still feel flat once you are standing inside it, and that gap matters wherever a client or reviewer needs to trust a space before it gets built. This paper walks through the design and construction of an immersive virtual landscape built specifically to test environmental legibility: the visual cues, layering, and scale references that let a viewer read distance and depth correctly.

The findings now shape every spatial environment built since, including the terrarium world in BioTerrarium.

Research study · iSpace Lab, SFU

Testing Cybersickness in Passive and Active VR Locomotion

iSpace Research Lab (Bernhard Riecke, SFU) · SteamVR, HP Reverb G2 & Meta Quest

A study comparing passive and active locomotion using the VR sickness benchmark built by the iSpace Research Lab at Simon Fraser University. The benchmark measures body sway before and after movement, moves participants through a virtual environment while they self-report cybersickness in real time, can toggle mitigation techniques (field-of-view reduction, dynamic blur, a virtual nose) mid-study, and automatically halts movement past a set discomfort threshold.

A research study run through the iSpace Lab rather than a solo build; forked on GitHub to preserve the exact version the study ran on.

Course research project · IAT 813, SFU

SocialEyes: Adaptive VR Social Skills Training for Autism

Bayesian AI · gaze-triggered virtual agents · Oculus Quest 3

A VR framework helping children with autism spectrum disorder practice social interaction with sixteen AI-driven virtual agents, each built around a distinct Myers-Briggs personality type, who respond only when a child actually looks at them. A Bayesian network governs how each agent reacts to gaze and to the behavior of the agent nearest it; six scientifically validated facial expressions give children clear emotional cues; Oculus Quest 3 eye tracking resolves exactly which agent is being looked at.

To validate the AI, a Monte Carlo simulation of thousands of gaze and gesture sequences was compared against probabilities computed directly from the Bayesian network — for example a 24 percent simulated rate against a 25 percent computed rate for one gesture, confirming the logic behaved as designed.

Still a proof of concept rather than a deployed clinical tool. Next step: real-time facial expression recognition, so agents can respond to a child’s own face, not just where they are looking.
