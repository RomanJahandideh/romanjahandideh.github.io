Research projects and publications

Designing responsive environments means negotiating a body's signals, a space's legibility, and how a person actually moves through both. My work sits across physiological computing and biofeedback interfaces, embodied interaction in virtual and game environments, and perception research into how depth, distance, and locomotion read inside a headset, always tested with real participants and real sensors, not left as a working prototype. The projects below are what that practice has produced: the systems I designed and built, the studies I ran on them, and the papers that came out of it. Some are published, one is working its way back through review, and one did not make it past review at all. I think it is worth showing all three honestly, since that is what research actually looks like.

Submitted, ACM SIGGRAPH 2026

Embedding Physiological Responsiveness into Game World Dynamics

BioTerrarium · Unity, Apple Watch heart rate & HRV · within subjects study, n = 24

Most games ask a lot of a player's nervous system and give almost nothing back. I wanted to build a small world that pays attention to the body instead of asking the player to pay attention to a meter. BioTerrarium is a closed glass ecosystem, a kind of living terrarium, where fog, vegetation, and light respond in real time to the player's own heart rate and heart rate variability. When the player is calm, the terrarium clears and grows. When stress builds, the world fogs over, overgrows, and the light desaturates. Nothing is ever shown as a number, the environment simply reflects the state of the body back to the player.

Watch it in action

How it works

The system reads heart rate from an Apple Watch Series 7 over Bluetooth and Apple HealthKit, bridges it through a small Swift app, and streams it into Unity over a WebSocket with under 100 milliseconds of latency. A two minute resting baseline sets a personal reference for each player, and every reading afterward is normalized against that baseline and sorted into one of four zones, from Coherent Calm through Focused Arousal and Stress Escalation to Dysregulated Overdrive. Each zone maps to fog density, vegetation growth, light diffusion, and a shader based blur, updated ten times a second so the world always feels like it is breathing along with the player.

More from the environment

What it produced

I ran a within subjects study with 24 participants, comparing this bioadaptive terrarium against an identical environment that changed on a fixed schedule instead of responding to the body. Players in the bioadaptive condition had a lower average heart rate, noticeably higher heart rate variability, and spent close to 80 percent of their playtime in the two calmest zones. Afterward they also reported more mindful attention, more positive affect, less negative affect, and deeper flow. In interviews, people described the terrarium as something that was breathing back at them and that calmed when they calmed.

Where it stands

I submitted this work to ACM SIGGRAPH 2026. It went through a full round of reviewer feedback and revisions, and in the end the paper was not accepted. I still think the system and the study hold up, and I am looking at where this line of research fits next.

Reading a Remote Teammate's Body

Remote collaboration · ambient physiological awareness · ACM

BioTerrarium showed that a single player's heart rhythm can shape a world around them without ever putting a number on screen. This project asks what happens when a second person joins that loop. Reading a Remote Teammate's Body looks at synchronous remote collaboration, the kind of screen sharing, co-design, or joint problem solving that increasingly happens with a teammate who is only present through a call, and asks how that teammate's focus, strain, or calm might be conveyed ambiently instead of displayed as a chart.

How it works

It extends the same ambient reciprocity principle behind BioTerrarium, letting an environment quietly carry a trace of a collaborator's physiological state, into a two person, real time setting, so presence and strain can be sensed the way they would be in the same room, instead of read off a screen.

Where it stands

I submitted this work to an ACM venue. The reviewers asked for a revise and resubmit rather than an outright acceptance or rejection, which means the core idea held up but the paper needs another pass. I am working through that feedback now, with the resubmission planned for 2027.

Optimizing Eye-Tracking Locomotion in Virtual Environments

Virtual Landscape Design Lab, Istanbul Technical University (dir. Dr. Ikhwan Kim) · head-mounted display research

Most VR locomotion still relies on a thumbstick or a teleport point, and both break the sense of presence a virtual landscape is trying to build. This project looks at using eye tracking itself as the locomotion input, letting where a person looks help guide where and how they move through a space, so exploring an environment feels closer to looking around a real place than operating a control scheme.

How it works

Building on the environmental legibility work from Creating Immersive Virtual Landscapes, this study compares different eye tracking locomotion schemes for comfort, accuracy, and how naturally they let someone explore a head-mounted VR environment, aimed at walkthroughs a client can move through without a tutorial.

Where it stands

I submitted this work to CAADRIA 2024, the Conference on Computer-Aided Architectural Design Research in Asia.

Creating Immersive Virtual Landscapes

Virtual Landscape Design Lab, Istanbul Technical University (dir. Dr. Ikhwan Kim) · depth perception & environmental legibility

During my Master's research in landscape architecture, I worked in the Virtual Landscape Design Lab, led by Dr. Ikhwan Kim at Istanbul Technical University, Türkiye, studying how people actually perceive depth and distance inside a head-mounted display. A virtual landscape can look right and still feel flat once you are standing inside it, and that gap matters for architectural and planning work, where a client or a reviewer needs to trust a space before it gets built.

How it works

This paper walks through the design and construction of an immersive virtual landscape built specifically to test environmental legibility: the visual cues, layering, and scale references that let a viewer read distance and depth correctly once they put on a headset.

What it produced

The findings from this study now shape how I build every spatial environment since, including the terrarium world in BioTerrarium, where legibility, not just visual fidelity, decides whether an environment reads correctly to the person standing inside it.

Research study · iSpace Lab, SFU

Testing Cybersickness in Passive and Active VR Locomotion

iSpace Research Lab (Bernhard Riecke, SFU) · SteamVR, HP Reverb G2 & Meta Quest

Cybersickness is still one of the biggest things standing between VR and comfortable, everyday use, and how a person moves through a virtual space, whether they are actively walking it or being moved through it, changes how sick that movement makes them feel. I ran a study comparing passive and active locomotion using the VR sickness benchmark built by the iSpace Research Lab at Simon Fraser University, under Bernhard Riecke.

How it works

The benchmark measures body sway before and after movement with eyes open and closed, then moves the participant through a virtual environment while they self report their cybersickness level in real time on a controller. It can also toggle common mitigation techniques on or off mid study, including static and dynamic field of view reduction, dynamic blur, and a virtual nose, and it automatically halts movement if a participant crosses a set discomfort threshold. I used this system to compare how passive and active locomotion differ in the cybersickness they produce, and where those mitigation techniques help most.

Where it stands

This was a research study run through the iSpace Lab rather than a solo build. The benchmark itself is the lab's own open source tool, and I have forked it on GitHub to keep a record of the exact version my study ran on.

Course research project · IAT 813, SFU

SocialEyes: Adaptive VR Social Skills Training for Autism

Bayesian AI · gaze triggered virtual agents · Oculus Quest 3

Coming from a design background, artificial intelligence used to feel like the part of this field I was most afraid of, too technical and too far from what I knew. SocialEyes is what happened when I decided to face that instead of avoiding it. It is a virtual reality framework that helps children with autism spectrum disorder practice social interaction with sixteen AI driven virtual agents, each built around a distinct Myers Briggs personality type, who respond only when the child actually looks at them.

How it works

A Bayesian network sits behind every agent, deciding how it reacts to a gaze and to the behavior of the agent nearest to it, so responses stay consistent with that agent's personality while never feeling scripted or repetitive. Six scientifically validated facial expressions, happiness, sadness, surprise, anger, fear, and neutral, give children clear emotional cues to read and respond to. Oculus Quest 3's eye tracking detects exactly which agent a child is looking at, so only the nearest agent responds at a time, keeping interactions structured instead of overwhelming.

What it produced

The task asks a child to find five social friends among thirty two agents, turning practice into something with a goal rather than an open ended simulation. To validate the AI itself, I ran a Monte Carlo simulation of thousands of agent gaze and gesture sequences and compared the resulting probabilities against the same values computed directly from the Bayesian network. They matched closely, for example a 24 percent simulated rate against a 25 percent computed rate for one gesture, confirming that the probabilistic logic underneath the agents was internally consistent and behaving the way it was designed to.

Where it stands

This started as a project for IAT 813 and is still a proof of concept rather than a deployed clinical tool, limited for now to gaze based interaction and a classroom sized environment. The next step I want to take is adding real time facial expression recognition, so the agents can respond to a child's own face and not just where they are looking.

Independent project · interaction design prototype

A Portfolio You Walk Through: Gamifying Access to Information

Browser game · p5.js, WebGL · progressive disclosure through level design

Most dense information, a CV, a regulation, a dataset, asks a reader to get through it all at once, flattening years or layers of context into one long static page. This project asks whether spatial, level based interaction can do better: instead of reading a CV top to bottom, a visitor walks through it, one architectural level at a time, and each level unlocks the next only once its own small spatial puzzle is solved.

How it works

Built as a browser game in p5.js and WebGL, it restructures a twelve chapter personal and professional history, Origins through Now, into twelve architectural levels rendered in this stark monochrome isometric style. A player walks rather than scrolls through each one. A split screen dual world mechanic lets two parallel states of the same space, different palettes, lighting, and visible objects, exist side by side, so a single level can hold two versions of the same information at once instead of splitting them across separate pages. Completing a level's spatial puzzle is what grants access to the next chapter's data, turning something normally skimmed into a structure a visitor has to move through to actually reach.

More from the level design

Environment as Interface

The underlying question reaches past a CV. Any dense, rule-bound document, a contract, a building code, a technical manual, a regulation, is functionally illegible to most of the people it actually governs, not because the information is secret, but because reading it in full, start to finish, was never a realistic way to access it. Making that kind of information spatial and interactive instead, something a person walks through, tests against a real case, and revises, rather than skims once and sets aside, is one way to make it legible to someone who was never going to read the document straight through. This project is a small, personal proof of that idea: information that has to be walked through instead of skimmed changes who can actually reach it.
