Vancouver View Corridor Massing

Rhino & Grasshopper study · deterministic measurements · City of Vancouver view corridor data

This project develops a computational method for generating building massing beneath Vancouver's protected view corridors. Unlike a flat height limit, a view corridor imposes a maximum geodetic elevation that rises with distance from a protected viewpoint, creating a sloped geometric ceiling. A building can therefore satisfy its ordinary zoning envelope and still be cut diagonally by the corridor. The study uses the City's official data to model this real constraint and ask what is gained by doing the geometry precisely.

Evaluation and Key Findings

The study compares precise floor-by-floor trimming against a conservative shortcut that caps the entire building at the lowest corridor elevation found on the site. For the Creekside Park J1 corridor and representative site, the flat-cap method produces 10 floors and 18,360 square metres of gross floor area; precise trimming produces 18 floors and 25,608 square metres, recovering 39.5 percent more usable area. A second study rotates a fixed-area footprint through seven orientations. Total floor area varies by only 0.02 percent, while floor count changes from 18 to 17 floors. The measured result is explained by the linear ceiling: the average of a linear height function depends on the footprint centroid, so rotation changes floor configuration without materially changing total volume.

Data and Geometry

The corridor is fetched from Vancouver's public Open Data Portal, with a verified offline copy as a fallback. The system derives the triangle's apex and sightline axis programmatically, converts geographic coordinates to local planar metres, computes the zoning footprint through the same tested half-plane intersection method used in the Parametric Massing Generator, and clips the footprint at each floor against the rising ceiling. The result is written as a Rhino 3DM file containing the corridor, site outlines, and one solid mesh per compliant floor.

Design Application

Because total floor area remains nearly constant while floor count changes, orientation can be selected for silhouette, floor-plate efficiency, or program rather than yield alone. A separate AI layer receives the already-computed rotation results and interprets them against an open design brief, such as a preference for fewer, larger floors. It recommends and explains a choice without calculating or altering the underlying figures.

Verification and Limits

The apex derivation, polygon clipping, and floor-by-floor trimming were tested independently. The generated 3DM file was checked for floor count, layer structure, and mesh vertex and face counts, while the rotation study was compared with a hand-derived continuous-volume formula. The Grasshopper component is written against the documented RhinoCommon API, but its final geometry-construction step was not executed because Rhino was unavailable in the authoring environment; that limitation is stated explicitly.
